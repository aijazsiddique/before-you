The dangerous moment

The riskiest career decision you'll ever make is the one made at your worst hour.

Resignation emails get drafted late at night, by people who are exhausted, angry, scared — and missing the information a decision this size deserves. Leaving feels like the only action large enough to match how bad you feel. The feeling deserves attention. The email does not have to be sent tonight.

And “stay forever” is not the only alternative. Before You Resign exists for the space between those two: the weeks where you slow the decision down, reduce this week's pressure, and find out what the facts actually say.

The advice you keep hearing

  • “Push through.” — from people who've never seen your 2 a.m.
  • “Just set boundaries.” — as if the other person weren't holding the deadline.
  • “Take a vacation.” — rest genuinely helps; it changes how you feel, not what the job requires.

The method

Three parts. In the right order.

The book is built so each part makes the next one possible — and so you can stop at any point and still have something useful.

Part I

Reduce the immediate pressure

Chapters 1–3
  • Make tonight smaller — a ten-minute landing for your worst evenings, and a two-minute three-line note that separates today's incident from tomorrow's decision.
  • Name the pressure accurately — is this workload, control, support, relationships, conflicting managers, work fit, or life outside work?
  • Make the workload visible — a capacity page that turns “I have too much work” into a concrete trade-off.
Part II

Change what happens at work

Chapters 4–7
  • Say what can actually be done — 11 practical scripts, with lower-risk alternatives where appropriate and a “before you send” checklist.
  • Have one useful conversation — a meeting card, plus follow-ups for the six responses managers actually give.
  • Create room to recover — rest, leave with limited disclosure, and a return that's actually protected.
  • Give repair a fair, limited test — conditions, a review date, and what you'll do if the changes never happen.
Part III

Make the next career decision

Chapters 8–12
  • Compare five real paths — stay with changes, leave or adjustments, internal transfer, search while employed, prepared resignation.
  • Put the financial facts on one page — runway, stress tests, cash timing.
  • Move without a second crisis — searches, interviews, resignations, counteroffers.
  • Bring your household into the plan — and finish with a 30-day plan and a decision dashboard.

Worksheet 2 · ~8 minutes

The Pressure Map

Not every bad job is the same bad job. The map covers nine sources of pressure drawn from occupational-health guidance — work volume, lack of control, missing support, relationships, conflicting responsibilities, change, physical conditions, work fit, life outside work. You pick the two worth acting on first, attach a recent example to each, and name who can actually change it. “I hate my job” becomes: the manager changed the priority three times this week and moved no deadlines. That's a problem with an owner.

Actual page · Worksheet 3
Worksheet 3 · ~10 minutes

The Capacity Page

“I have too much work” is a feeling. The capacity page makes it arithmetic: scheduled hours, fixed meetings and breaks, realistic interruptions, time left for planned work — and the gap. Then you choose what changes: delay, delegate, reduce, or stop. A worked example shows exactly how it plays out: 40 scheduled hours, but only about 20 left for planned work once meetings, breaks and recurring requests are accounted for — against 26 hours of planned work. That's a six-hour gap, and a proposal a manager can answer with a yes, a no, or a counter-offer.

Actual page · Worksheet 6
Worksheet 6 · ~12 minutes

The Repair Agreement

“Things will improve” is not a plan; it's a promise with no expiry date. The repair agreement turns it into a bounded test: the required change, who can make it happen, the start date, what will show it happened, a review date — and, in writing, what you will do if the changes do not happen. You are not required to test an unsafe situation; the guide is explicit about that. But where repair is plausible, this is how you find out instead of waiting indefinitely.

“I need [this] to change by [date]. If it does not, my next step is [step].”

Your Runway Page

The financial facts, on one page, in any currency: usable transition money, essential monthly spending, reliable income during the gap, monthly shortfall, approximate runway. Then the guide stress-tests your hopeful version and walks through cash timing, employer health coverage end dates, and repayment clauses to check before giving notice. Financial fear feeds on vagueness; this chapter feeds it facts — a full worked example included, clearly labelled a worked example, not a target.

The 30-Day Plan & the Decision Dashboard

The plan breaks the month into four windows — one main action each, from “address immediate needs” to “review options and money” — with what you'll have afterwards. The decision dashboard compresses everything into six evidence questions, ticked for what you've observed, not what you hope. Worksheet 10 turns the result into a dated record: your next step, your constraint, your fallback, your review date. Thirty days is a planning window, not a deadline for your entire career — the guide says that too.

One rule sits above every path — stay, change or leave: if health or safety requires immediate action, that comes first. The guide says so on page 5, not in a footnote.

What changes

Whatever you decide, you'll decide it on purpose.

Tonight

  • One named problem instead of a fog of dread
  • One small action you can actually finish
  • Permission to stop for the night — the decision gets an appointment, not a deadline of “now”
  • A ten-minute landing: water, food, a quieter hour, the phone face down
  • The resignation email stays a private draft — information kept, decision not sent

In 30 days — your case file

  • A pressure map with the two problems worth acting on
  • A capacity page with a trade-off a manager can answer
  • A record of what was agreed — and what actually happened
  • A repair test with conditions and a review date
  • Five financial numbers on one page
  • A chosen next step, a fallback, and the next review date

That's not a mood. That's evidence. Some people finish this guide and stay — because the work genuinely changed. Some leave — because the evidence said so. Both are good outcomes. The bad outcome is another year of deciding at 1 a.m.

Straight answers

The questions you're already asking.

“What if I already know I want to quit?”
Then use the guide to plan the exit instead of improvising it: the runway page, the facts to verify before giving notice, resignation wording, counteroffer checks, and a first-week-after plan. Knowing you want to leave is when a responsible exit becomes possible — not when planning stops.
“What if I can't afford to resign?”
That's exactly why the financial runway and the staged decision process exist. You may find you can't afford to quit yet — and, more usefully, what would have to change: the shortfall, the timing, the interim income, the review threshold. Vague money fear paralyzes; numbers can be planned around.
“What if the problem is my manager, not my career?”
The guide separates problems at different levels — tasks, manager, team capacity, company-wide expectations, a temporary peak, or genuine work fit — because each has a different fix. The pressure map and the repair test tell them apart with evidence, so you don't quit a career over one manager, or stay for a company that wouldn't do the same for you.
“I barely have energy to read a book.”
It's built for that state. The shortest route is three steps: the safety page, a two-minute three-line note, and one script. Every substantial worksheet has a deliberately smaller version. One page tonight; the rest when you have more energy.
“Will this tell me whether to quit?”
No — and that's the point. A book that told you to quit would be guessing about your savings, your health, your contract and your household. This guide helps you obtain the information a decision this size deserves, then you make the call — with evidence, a fallback and a review date.
“Is this therapy, medical or legal advice?”
No. It's an educational guide to workplace decisions — not diagnosis, treatment, or individual medical, legal or financial advice. Where health or safety matters, it directs you to professional support, with crisis lines for six regions and a note you can bring to a doctor's appointment if words are difficult.
“What if my workplace is genuinely unsafe or abusive?”
Then you are not required to run a “repair experiment” — the guide says so explicitly. Where safety, severe symptoms or retaliation are concerns, it prioritizes support routes, escalation and accurate documentation over scripts. A decision guide can't fix a dangerous workplace; it can help you stop doubting what you're seeing and act sooner.
